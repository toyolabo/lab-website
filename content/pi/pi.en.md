## Short Introduction

Prof. Toyo (Toyotaro) Suzumura is a professor at the University of Tokyo since April 2021. He also serves as a visiting professor at Barcelona Supercomputing Center in Spain. His current research interests are graph neural networks, large language models, and their application to e-commerce, medical science, finance, and such, and high-performance computing/supercomputing. After obtaining a Ph.D. in computer science in 2004 from Tokyo Institute of Technology, he has 17-year experiences from 2004 to 2021 in IBM Research as a principal research scientist in global research labs including Tokyo (2004-2013), Dublin (2013-2015), and IBM Thomas J. Watson Research Center and MIT-IBM Watson AI Lab in New York (2015-2021). As for his research achievements, he won an international competition related to large-scale graph processing named Graph500 10 times since 2014. He has published more than 90 papers to top-tier international conferences such as AAAI/RecSys/WWW/VLDB/Supercomputing, and journals and served as a sponsor co-chair of AAAI 2024, a PC chair of IEEE BigData 2017 as well as serving program committee members in numerous international conferences such as AAAI, ACM/IEEE Supercomputing, and IEEE Cloud.

## Full Biography

He is a full professor at the Graduate School of Information Science and Technology, the University of Tokyo since April 1st, 2021. Until March 2021 - prior to moving to academia, he was a principal research scientist and global lead of scalable graph learning project and the Future of Computing initiative at the headquarter of IBM Research, namely IBM T.J. Watson Research Center located in New York, USA. He led the worldwide team of more than 50 world-class top scientists in the area of artificial intelligence, big data, high-performance computing, large-scale graph analytics in IBM Research and led the Research Strategy for Financial Service Research as well. He also served as a principal investigator for the MIT-IBM Watson AI Lab project with Prof. Charles Leiserson from MIT Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory (CSAIL). He currently also serves as a visiting full professor at the national laboratory in Spain – named Barcelona Supercomputing Center in Spain.

He has broad knowledge and experience in the research and development of artificial intelligence, machine learning, large-scale graph analytics/learning, broader coverage of computer science areas including big data processing in large-scale distributed systems, parallel and distributed middleware, and its application to real-time data stream computing, large-scale graph processing, cloud computing, and various performance optimization technologies, design and implementation of dynamic scripting languages and object-oriented parallel language and large-scale traffic simulation.

Prof. Suzumura had worked for IBM Research since 2004 for 17 years after finishing his Ph.D. in 2004 in the area of Cloud/Grid and supercomputing fields at Tokyo Institute of Technology in Japan. During his 17 years in IBM Research, as a dual-appointment, he had been also appointed as a visiting associate professor at Tokyo Institute of Technology since 2009 and supervising 16 graduate students in his own laboratory "Suzumura Laboratory" and also a visiting associate professor of University College Dublin in Ireland since 2014/04 supervising 10 master students and 3 Ph.D. students. Since 2013/10, He had worked for IBM Research – Ireland, the R&D headquarter of IBM Research in Europe as an international assignment specifically focusing on a variety of Smarter-Cities related technologies. Since April 2015, He joined the headquarter of IBM Research named IBM T.J. Watson Research Center in New York, the USA as a full-time research scientist.

He has published over 90 reviewed research papers including top-quality international conferences and workshops as well as serving program committee chairs and members for top-tier big data and AI conferences. His notable accomplishment is that he won the 1st place in the Graph500 supercomputing ranking ten times.

## Current Research Overview (April 2021 – present): AI, Graph Neural Networks, Recommender Systems, Large Language Models and High-Performance Computing

Since moving to the University of Tokyo in April 2021, he has been conducting fundamental and applied research in artificial intelligence, with a particular focus on graph neural networks (GNNs). He focuses on large-scale networks that change dynamically, and develops new GNN models that exploit their multi-scale nature in time and space. On the application side, he mainly targets recommender systems and matching problems through joint research with industry, including product recommendation in e-commerce, recommendation for job matching, news recommendation, and route recommendation in road traffic networks. Since 2023 he has also been working on applying large language models to recommender systems.

### 1. Recommender systems built on graph neural networks, reinforcement learning and large language models (2021 – present)

Together with TOYOTA (automotive), Nikkei Inc. (news platform), SMS Co., Ltd. (job platform for healthcare and nursing care) and Rakuten (e-commerce platform), he conducts research on recommender systems using the latest AI technologies. The work on news recommendation was accepted at RecSys 2023, the top conference in the field, where it was selected as one of three Best Paper Candidates among submissions from major platform companies such as Google and Amazon, and received the Best Student Paper Award. ### 2. Integrating large-scale graph analytics with machine learning and deep learning, and its application and commercialization in finance (2009 – present)

From 2015 to 2021 he lived in New York and, as global lead of the "Scalable Graph Learning" project at IBM T.J. Watson Research Center, the headquarters of IBM Research, tackled a wide range of industrial problems. For anti-money-laundering in finance, he proposed Graph-Powered Machine Learning, which integrates large-scale graph analytics with machine and deep learning: graph features are extracted from billions of transactions with high-speed graph analytics and combined with other statistical features for machine learning, improving the detection rate by 30% over conventional methods. This was commercialized in May 2018 as IBM Financial Crimes Insight with Watson (Graph Analytics), the world's first product applying large-scale graph processing to financial crime detection, built on a body of graph analytics research. The work received the Best Paper Award at IEEE HiPC 2018 and was published as an invited paper and at top AI and database conferences including AAAI 2020 and VLDB 2019.

### 3. Scalable large-scale graph analytics algorithms on supercomputers

Graphs, composed of vertices and edges, are applicable to many domains such as social networks, finance and cybersecurity. While single-node algorithm optimization had made graphs with millions of vertices tractable, graphs with hundreds of millions to trillions of vertices now need to be processed. He carried out pioneering research on fast and scalable graph processing on distributed-memory machines such as supercomputers. In Graph500, the biannual competition for parallel and distributed graph algorithms on supercomputers, his team ranked 3rd in the world in 2012 using the University of Tokyo's Oakleaf-FX and 1st in the world ten times between 2014 and 2019 using RIKEN's K computer. The team proposed graph algorithm optimizations that exploit supercomputer hardware characteristics and performance optimizations based on properties of graph data structures, with papers accepted at top conferences on high-performance large-scale processing such as HPDC and BigData. These algorithms are also used in the Graph500 runs on the fastest supercomputer "Fugaku" and contributed substantially to its 1st-place ranking.

He also implemented and open-sourced ScaleGraph, a large-scale graph processing library that end users can apply to graphs with billions of vertices. Using ScaleGraph, he collected a huge Twitter graph of 460 million vertices and 28 billion edges, ten times larger than the data used in the state-of-the-art research at the time, and analyzed the temporal evolution of this large-scale network, obtaining new findings such as transitions in community density over time by country and language. This work was accepted at WWW (World Wide Web), the top conference in web technology.

### 4. Large-scale social simulation platforms and their application to urban traffic (2009 – 2015)

Agent-based models are effective for modeling complex phenomena such as traffic simulation for urban policy and epidemic simulation in epidemiology. Simulation platforms for agent models had been limited to around one million agents. This research developed a scalable agent simulation platform targeting more than one billion agents that runs faster than real time. It proposed performance optimizations that prevent slowdowns caused by workload imbalance across machines in distributed-memory systems and by latency in synchronous execution models. Based on these optimizations, a billion-agent simulation was run ten times faster than real time on 128 nodes of Tokyo Tech's TSUBAME supercomputer. The results were accepted at top simulation conferences such as WSC and ACM PADS.

These results were used as the traffic simulation platform across IBM Research's global labs (Ireland, Brazil, Australia, Kenya and Japan). At IBM Research's European lab in Dublin, Ireland, a joint project with local government agencies applied it to relieving traffic congestion, demonstrating its usefulness in practice.

### 5. Large-scale parallel and distributed data stream processing (2009 – 2013)

One challenging problem in real-time processing of streaming data arriving continuously from a large number of geographically distributed sensors is how to use computing resources efficiently while guaranteeing the real-time requirements of applications when the arrival rate of the stream data changes. This research proposed a method that predicts the computing resources required according to the data rate and dynamically provisions resources on cloud infrastructure based on the prediction. For general applications consisting of both batch and stream processing, it proposed a method that dynamically allocates limited computing resources between stream and batch processing according to the changing data rate while respecting real-time requirements. It further proposed methods for dynamically transforming data-flow graphs of stream programs according to the data rate and the performance characteristics of each resource, and algorithms and a general-purpose runtime for real-time processing of dynamic graph structures built from stream data. Many papers from this work were accepted at top conferences such as IEEE BigData, ACM WWW, IEEE IPDPS and IEEE Cloud.

In practice, he contributed substantially to bringing large-scale systems based on stream processing into production. For NTT's FLET'S Hikari Light service, targeting 50 million subscribers with complex billing logic that differs per subscriber and simultaneous fraud detection, he led the design of a real-time billing software system from concept to implementation, providing solutions to operational challenges such as performance and fault tolerance.

### 6. Grid computing (1999 – 2004)

In grid computing, which presents the infrastructure of multiple organizations with heterogeneous computing resources, security policies and job scheduling policies as a single virtual system, a challenging research problem is how to autonomously discover and manage the vast number of distributed computing services available on the grid. This research developed a method for autonomous service discovery and management using Jini, a distributed object technology running on each organization's systems, proposed a software architecture for centrally managing the state of those services across the grid, and designed and implemented a system called JiPANG. Its novelty was recognized with acceptance at Supercomputing 2001, the most prestigious international conference in supercomputing. JiPANG was also applied to and extended for grid service implementations at the University of Tennessee, Indiana University and UC San Diego, demonstrating its usefulness, and its design philosophy was recognized as a reference architecture by the Open Grid Forum standardization body.

## Essays

### My Current Policy on Research Themes (as of October 2021)

This is an open-ended topic that every researcher should deeply think about at all times. Let me take a note of my current policy as of Oct 18, 2021, with regards to how I can decide research themes. This policy might change every year, and I believe that this should be.

Before fully joining academia in April 2021, I had been with IBM Research for 17 years after I got my Ph.D. in 2004. And also in the past 8 years, I had been in Europe and United States. I learned a lot from one of the top computer science research institutes - IBM Research. It is well known that it is difficult to continue to maintain basic research labs in any industry since one of the important KPIs for industries is profits. Balancing profits and pursuing basic research is a pretty challenging problem.

From my experiences at IBM Research, research problems and themes had been decided by thoroughly discussing what could be groundbreaking things for our society and also their clients. Thus, research themes and directions were decided in a top-down manner. Sometimes innovative research ideas should come in a bottom-up manner without thinking of any real applications in society, but this might be rare. If you think about what computer science is, it should always be tied to its applications and impacts on real-world societies. Any kind of outcome from computer science should be the fundamental basis for advancing real applications.

This is my current research style as of writing in April 2021, but nobody knows how it might change every year. Thus, even if I move to academia and supervise students and work with collaborators, one of the most important points for me is what could be real values for our real-world societies. We need to continue to ask ourselves all the time whether our research themes are not only for publishing to top-tier conferences but also give big impacts to societies. We should not set up research themes only for publications. This should be common sense that we can agree upon among senior researchers, but I can see this sometimes happening for young researchers who feel pressure to have more papers to move up to the next level of their careers. I will be continuing to add my thoughts in research styles, but there is no clear answer. That's for sure. That's why it is always important to continue to think about the reasons for what you are working on.

### My ongoing experiments in Tokyo as a global career (Nov, 2021) - shifting from Industry to fully Academia

After spending around 8 years in Europe and the United States, I decided to relocate to Tokyo from New York City due to the pandemic situation in 2020. Although it did not matter with COVID or not, I had been feeling that where you live and work does NOT matter for computer scientists. Thus I started to think about whether I should continue to stay in New York City where the pandemic gave strong damage. I managed to obtain a permanent residency or green card and was supposed to live and work in the US longer but the pandemic situation made me seriously start to think that I should not stick to the US and should relocate to a city where I can feel safer and comfortable to live and work.

Around June 2020, I decided to relocate to Tokyo. Then after exploring some job opportunities, I got back to Tokyo at the end of March 2021. Although I finally went to the US and worked there - especially for the respectful (still love it) research institute - IBM T.J. Watson Research Center, the headquarter of IBM Research, which I was dreaming of for a long time since I was a grad student. One aspect of my goals got achieved in that I was able to do and lead various exciting projects that I had thought of, but my intuition told me that I should move to the next step in my career.

Now I am in Tokyo. To me, as previously mentioned, where you live does not matter and should not matter. So wherever you live, you can connect to respectful researchers all over the world if you look in the same direction - using online communication tools obviously - without working with them at the same physical location. Life is a journey and nobody can change or ask how and where you live. It's your life. I will enjoy life and work in Tokyo at this moment. (Noted: Nov 2021, Tokyo)
